Every investment process goes through periods of loss. This is not a sign that something is broken; it is a property of taking risk at all. The more useful question is not whether a drawdown will happen, but how it will be handled when it does.

The difficulty is that drawdowns are when judgement is least reliable. Losses create pressure to act: to abandon a rule that has stopped working, to increase size to recover faster, or to stop trading altogether. Each of these can be the right decision. Each can also be the worst one, and in the moment it is very hard to tell which.

Our answer is to make as many of those decisions as possible in advance, while things are calm.

**Risk is sized before the trade, not after.** Position sizes and overall exposure are set by rules that do not depend on recent results. We do not increase risk to make back a loss.

**We know what normal looks like.** Before a rule is used, we study how deep and how long its historical losses have been. That gives us a reference point. A drawdown within that range is uncomfortable but expected. One well beyond it is a signal to investigate.

**Investigation comes before intervention.** When results fall outside what we expected, the first step is to understand why: whether the market has changed, whether the rule has a flaw we did not see, or whether this is simply an unlucky stretch. Changes to rules go through the same written, tested process as new ones. They are not made mid-stream in response to a bad week.

**We communicate early.** Investors should hear about a difficult period from us, plainly and promptly, not discover it in a statement.

None of this removes losses. It does mean that during the periods when it is hardest to think clearly, most of the important decisions have already been made.
