---
title: Rules before capital
date: 2026-09-15
summary: Why every idea we act on is written down, in full, before it is tested, and long before it is funded.
draft: true
---

Most investment ideas begin as an impression. A chart looks a certain way, a company seems undervalued, a pattern feels familiar. Impressions are useful as a starting point, but they make a poor basis for a decision, because they cannot be examined. An impression has no edges. It can always be reinterpreted after the fact to fit whatever happened.

Our first discipline is to remove that flexibility. Before an idea is tested, it is written down as a complete set of rules: what has to be true for a position to be taken, how large that position can be, and what has to happen for it to be closed. If a rule cannot be stated precisely enough for someone else to apply it and reach the same answer, it is not yet a rule.

This sounds bureaucratic. In practice it does three things.

**It makes ideas testable.** A written rule can be applied consistently to historical data, and its behaviour can be studied: how often it acts, how it behaves in different market conditions, where it fails. An impression cannot.

**It makes failure informative.** When a written rule underperforms, we can ask whether the rule was wrong, whether the conditions changed, or whether it was never robust in the first place. Each answer leads somewhere. When an impression underperforms, there is very little to learn.

**It separates research from conviction.** Testing is not a search for confirmation. Most rules we write do not survive it, and that is the intended outcome. A process that approves most of what it examines is not filtering anything.

Historical testing has well-known limits. Markets change, and a rule that fits the past too closely will often disappoint in the future. We treat testing as a way of discarding weak ideas rather than as a forecast of how strong ideas will perform, and we hold the surviving rules to further scrutiny before they are used with real capital.

None of this guarantees a result. It does mean that when we act, we know exactly what we are doing and why, and that we will be able to tell, afterwards, whether it worked for the reasons we expected.
