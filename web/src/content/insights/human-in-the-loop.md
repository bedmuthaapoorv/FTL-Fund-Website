title: Why a person still signs off

summary: A systematic process benefits from a human checkpoint, provided the checkpoint has a narrow and well-defined job.

There is a common view that a systematic process should run untouched. If the rules are good, the argument goes, any human involvement can only introduce bias.

We agree with the concern and disagree with the conclusion. Every trade in our process is reviewed and signed off by a person before it is placed. The value of that step depends entirely on keeping its job narrow.

**What the review is for.** The reviewer checks that the inputs are sound and that the output makes operational sense: that the data a signal relies on is complete and current, that a corporate action or trading halt has not distorted it, that the order is the size the rules intended, and that nothing about the instrument has changed in a way the rules could not have known. These are failures of plumbing, not of judgement, and they are exactly the failures automated systems are prone to repeat silently.

**What the review is not for.** The reviewer does not second-guess a valid signal because the market feels uncomfortable, or add a trade the rules did not produce. A human checkpoint that drifts into discretionary trading undermines the reason for having rules at all. Where a review stops a trade, the reason is recorded, so that over time we can see whether the checkpoint is catching errors or quietly overriding the process.

**Why it matters more when things go wrong.** Most of the time the review is uneventful, and it should be. Its value shows up on the rare days when data arrives late, a symbol changes, or the market behaves in a way no historical sample contained. On those days, a person who understands both the rules and their limits is a better safeguard than any additional layer of code.

A systematic process and human oversight are not opposites. The rules provide consistency; the review keeps that consistency from becoming blindness.
